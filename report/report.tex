% ── Abstract ────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
\noindent
When bad weather hits a spoke airport on the American Airlines network, the
disruption rarely stays local.  A delayed inbound flight into DFW compresses
the crew's turnaround window, pushes back the outbound departure, and can
cascade into downstream failures across entirely different routes.  This
project asks whether those fragile combinations can be identified before crew
sequences are built, so schedulers can route around the risk.

We model real $A \to \text{DFW} \to B$ aircraft rotations rather than
synthetic airport pairings, drawing on BTS on-time records (2015--2024),
ERA5 reanalysis weather from Open-Meteo covering all 204 DFW-connected
airports, derived cascade and crew-duty features, and DFW hub statistics.
We reconstruct 398,098 tail-matched sequences and train a calibrated XGBoost
classifier that estimates disruption risk at the corridor-month level.
Calibrated scores are directly interpretable as expected disruption rates.

The model achieves a validation AUC of \textbf{0.825} and Average Precision
of \textbf{0.445} on 2024 holdout data (positive rate 11.4\%,
$\mathrm{AP}_{\text{lift}} = 3.90\times$ above random).  It is deployed as
an interactive Streamlit dashboard with SHAP-based explanations and a
Hungarian-algorithm sequence optimizer.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Dallas/Fort Worth International Airport sits at the center of American
Airlines' hub-and-spoke network.  Its efficiency depends on tight connections:
aircraft, crews, and passengers flow through DFW on short turns, and schedules
are built assuming those turns will complete on time.  When weather disrupts
even one leg in that chain, the consequences propagate.  An inbound flight
arriving late into DFW compresses the crew's turnaround, delays the outbound
departure, and can push a crew toward their duty-time limit --- turning a
single storm event at a spoke airport into a multi-leg disruption.

The EPPS--American Airlines challenge asks teams to identify which
$A \to \text{DFW} \to B$ sequence pairings are most likely to fail under
weather stress, so schedulers can avoid building those sequences in the first
place.  This is not a weather forecasting problem.  It is a historical risk
characterization problem: given what we know about how airports, seasons, and
aircraft rotation chains behave, which corridor-month combinations are
structurally fragile?

\subsection{Key Idea: Model Real Rotations, Not Synthetic Pairings}

Our central methodological contribution is choosing the right unit of analysis.
The obvious approach --- pairing all inbound flights from $A$ with all outbound
flights to $B$ on the same day --- fabricates hundreds of synthetic sequences
that never happened.  Labels based on ``did any flight that day get disrupted?''
then scale with hub traffic volume, not with actual operational fragility.
Our MCO$\to$LAX June corridor received a 87\% risk score under this approach,
against an observed disruption rate of 11.6\%.

Instead, we match inbound and outbound legs by \emph{aircraft tail number}
within a realistic 30--240 minute turnaround window.  This reconstructs real
rotations --- the same physical aircraft, likely the same crew --- and produces
calibrated scores that actually track observed disruption.  The rest of the
system (feature engineering, calibration, optimizer) follows naturally from
this choice.

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Real-sequence modeling.}  398,098 tail-matched rotations; 11.4\%
        positive rate versus the 42\% fabricated by corridor-day cross-joining.
  \item \textbf{Full-coverage weather.}  ERA5 reanalysis via Open-Meteo eliminates
        the 42\% NaN rate from NOAA GSOM's incomplete station coverage.
  \item \textbf{Calibrated risk scores.}  Isotonic regression maps raw model
        outputs to observed disruption rates; scores are operationally readable.
  \item \textbf{Cascade and duty features.}  Delay propagation and crew-duty
        proxies capture operational fragility beyond meteorological exposure alone.
  \item \textbf{Decision-support deployment.}  Interactive dashboard with SHAP
        explanations and a Hungarian-algorithm sequence optimizer.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{BTS On-Time Performance (2015--2024)}

The BTS On-Time Performance database provides flight-level records for every
reported domestic flight: departure and arrival times, delay causes broken out
by category (weather, NAS, late-aircraft, carrier), aircraft tail numbers, and
cancellation codes.  We filter to flights touching DFW and use all-carrier
traffic --- airport weather fragility is not carrier-specific, and broader
coverage gives a richer statistical picture of each airport under stress.

Years 2020--2021 are excluded due to COVID-era distortions.  The year 2024 is
held out entirely for validation; the model sees no 2024 data during training.

\subsection{ERA5 Reanalysis Weather (Open-Meteo)}

Our original weather source, NOAA GSOM, relied on physical station networks:
only $\sim$55\% of airports had a mapped station, leaving 42\% of training
rows with missing weather features.  XGBoost's NaN routing learned a spurious
signal --- airports without stations were, on average, smaller and calmer ---
systematically under-scoring genuinely risky airports like Anchorage (ANC).

We resolved this by switching to \textbf{ERA5}~\cite{hersbach2020era5}, the
ECMWF global atmospheric reanalysis available at any coordinate on Earth via
the Open-Meteo archive API.  ERA5 gives consistent, complete records for all
204 DFW-connected airports --- zero missing values.  We retrieve monthly
summaries for 2015--2024 (24,480 airport-month records) and compute ten
climatological features per airport as stable seasonal baselines.

\subsection{DFW Hub Weather}

Every modeled sequence passes through DFW, so hub conditions are a shared
risk factor independent of the specific spoke pair.  We derive four monthly
statistics directly from BTS: weather-delay rate, weather-cancellation rate,
mean weather-delay minutes, and 95th-percentile delay minutes.

\section{Feature Engineering}
\label{sec:features}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Tail-Matched Sequence Construction}
\label{sec:tailmatch}

\begin{keybox}[title=Why Corridor-Day Cross-Joining is Flawed]
On a busy hub day with 8 inbound flights from airport $A$ and 6 outbound
flights to $B$, a cross-join fabricates $8 \times 6 = 48$ synthetic pairings,
none of which correspond to a real rotation.  If \emph{any} flight that day is
disrupted, the whole corridor-day is labeled risky --- the model learns hub
traffic volume $\approx$ risk.  Result: MCO$\to$LAX June scores 87\% against
an observed disruption rate of 11.6\%.
\end{keybox}

We instead match inbound and outbound legs by aircraft tail number on the same
flight date, enforcing a 30--240 minute turnaround window.  This reconstructs
real rotations: the same physical aircraft (and most likely the same crew)
operated both legs.  Result: 398,098 genuine rotations at 11.4\% observed
disruption rate (down from the fabricated 42\%).

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Tail-Matched Sequence Construction}
\label{alg:tailmatch}
\KwIn{BTS flight table $F$ (DFW-filtered, 2015--2024)}
\KwOut{Sequence table $S$ with one row per real aircraft rotation}
$\text{IB} \gets \{f \in F : f.\texttt{Dest} = \text{DFW}\}$ \tcp*{inbound legs}
$\text{OB} \gets \{f \in F : f.\texttt{Origin} = \text{DFW}\}$ \tcp*{outbound legs}
Pre-rename leg-specific columns: \texttt{ArrDelay\_A}, \texttt{WeatherDelay\_B}, etc.\;
$S \gets \text{IB} \bowtie_{\texttt{Tail\_Number},\,\texttt{FlightDate}} \text{OB}$\;
$S \gets \{s \in S : 30 \le \texttt{CRSDepTime\_B} - \texttt{CRSArrTime\_A} \le 240\}$\;
$S \gets \{s \in S : s.\texttt{Origin\_A} \ne s.\texttt{Dest\_B}\}$ \tcp*{no round-trips}
\Return $S$
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Target Variable}
\label{sec:target}

A rotation is \textbf{disrupted} if any of the following hold:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Weather delay $\ge 15$ min on the inbound leg ($A \to \text{DFW}$)
  \item Weather delay $\ge 15$ min on the outbound leg ($\text{DFW} \to B$)
  \item \textbf{Cascade:} inbound arrival delay $> 15$ min \emph{and}
        late-aircraft delay accounts for $\ge 50\%$ of the outbound departure delay
\end{itemize}

A corridor-month is \textbf{high-risk} ($y = 1$) when $>$25\% of rotations were disrupted:
\begin{equation}
  y = \mathbf{1}\!\left[\texttt{observed\_bad\_rate} > 0.25\right]
\end{equation}
Positive class: 11.4\% across 398,098 sequence-months.

\subsection{Score Calibration}
\label{sec:calibration}

Raw XGBoost outputs are ranking signals, not disruption rates.  We apply
isotonic regression to map raw scores monotonically to the observed bad-rate
scale:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{p}_{\text{cal}} = f_{\text{iso}}(\hat{p}_{\text{raw}}),
  \quad f_{\text{iso}} \text{ non-decreasing},
  \quad \hat{p}_{\text{cal}} \approx \overline{r}_{\text{bad}}
\end{equation}
The fit uses 309 breakpoints weighted by $\sqrt{n_{\text{sequences}}}$ so that
sparse corridors contribute less.  Output range: [1.2\%, 58.3\%].

\subsection{Handling Sparse Corridors}

Most corridor-months have only a few historical rotations; one disruption out
of three gives a volatile 33\% observed rate.  XGBoost's minimum-samples-per-split
smooths over small groups, and the calibration weighting further reduces their
influence.  The dashboard surfaces the rotation count behind each score.

\subsection{Inference Fallback}

ERA5 covers all 204 training airports.  For any new airport at inference time,
the predictor substitutes month-level population medians.  Without this fallback,
XGBoost would learn ``no ERA5 data $\approx$ lower risk'' and under-score
remote but genuinely risky airports.  Imputed features are flagged in the
dashboard's SHAP panel.

\section{Sequence Optimizer}
\label{sec:optimizer}
% ============================================================

Given a day's DFW schedule, the optimizer finds the assignment of inbound
arrivals to outbound departures that minimizes total calibrated risk.  This
is a linear assignment problem: each aircraft can be paired with at most one
outbound route, and each outbound route requires at most one aircraft.

\subsection{Hungarian Algorithm}

We build a cost matrix of calibrated risk scores over all feasible
(inbound, outbound) pairs, where feasibility requires a 30--240 minute
turnaround.  The Hungarian algorithm (\texttt{scipy.optimize.linear\_sum\_assignment})
solves the optimal assignment in $O(n^3)$ time, fast enough for DFW's typical
100--200 daily movements.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{DFW Sequence Optimizer}
\label{alg:optimizer}
\KwIn{Inbound flights $I$, outbound flights $O$, risk model $f$}
\KwOut{Optimal assignment $A^* \subseteq I \times O$}
$C \gets \mathbf{0}^{|I| \times |O|}$\;
\For{$(i, o) \in I \times O$}{
  \eIf{$30 \le \text{turnaround}(i,o) \le 240$}{
    $C[i, o] \gets f(i, o)$
  }{
    $C[i, o] \gets \infty$
  }
}
$(r^*, c^*) \gets \textsc{HungarianSolve}(C)$\;
\Return $A^* = \{(I[r^*_k], O[c^*_k])\}$
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Live Schedule Integration}

The optimizer sources the day's schedule in priority order:
\textbf{AviationStack API} (live, requires free API key) $\to$
\textbf{BTS 2024 analog} (same month + day-of-week from historical data) $\to$
\textbf{OpenSky Network} (free, $\sim$30\% AA coverage, last resort).
The BTS analog provides a realistic proxy for a typical operating day when no
live data is available.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Key Findings}

The most consistent signal in the model is the interaction between seasonal
timing, hub congestion, and airport-level propagation tendency.  Summer
corridors connecting two storm-prone spoke airports are high-risk not simply
because either endpoint is individually volatile, but because they tend to be
simultaneously stressed and because DFW amplifies rather than absorbs that
compound exposure.  Cascade amplification features
(\texttt{tc\_cascade\_amplif\_mean}, \texttt{A\_ap\_cascade\_rate}) are
consistently among the top predictors --- an airport that reliably absorbs a
late inbound scores very differently from one that propagates the delay,
even under identical weather conditions.  The tail-matched framing makes this
signal usable: with a true 3.6 rotations per corridor-month on average, the
disruption rate is a meaningful statistic rather than an artifact of hub volume.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tail-number coverage.}  Crew swaps and aircraft substitutions
        break the tail chain; estimated coverage $\sim$65--70\% of actual AA sequences.
  \item \textbf{Monthly weather granularity.}  ERA5 features are monthly averages;
        a daily-join version would provide flight-date-specific conditions.
  \item \textbf{Calibration on full dataset.}  Isotonic regression fitted on all
        398k rows; calibration error on genuinely new route-months will be slightly higher.
  \item \textbf{2025 data not yet incorporated.}  The training window currently
        ends at December 2024.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Conclusion}

Disruptions at DFW do not respect the boundaries of individual flights.
They cascade through aircraft rotations, compress crew turns, and create
ripple effects that schedulers cannot always anticipate.  By shifting the
unit of analysis from synthetic corridor-days to real tail-matched rotations,
we produce a risk score that reflects actual operational fragility rather
than hub traffic volume.  The system achieves AUC 0.825 and
$\mathrm{AP}_{\text{lift}} = 3.90\times$ on 2024 holdout data, with
calibrated scores that track observed disruption rates closely.

The broader value is operational: a corridor-month score that means
``12 out of 100 rotations historically disrupted'' gives schedulers a concrete
basis for buffering sequences, prioritizing recovery resources, and avoiding
the most fragile pairings during high-risk seasons.  The optimizer translates
that insight into a concrete recommendation --- a lower-risk assignment of
aircraft to routes --- that can be acted on without any modeling expertise
from the scheduler.
