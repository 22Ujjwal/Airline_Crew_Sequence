% ── Abstract ────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Weather disruptions at a hub airport rarely stay local. At Dallas/Fort Worth
International Airport (DFW), a delayed inbound flight can compress a crew turn,
push back an outbound departure, and trigger downstream reliability problems
across the network. This project asks whether those vulnerable pairings can be
identified before crews are assigned, so that American Airlines can avoid
building especially fragile sequences in the first place.

We approach the problem by modeling real $A \to \text{DFW} \to B$ rotations
rather than synthetic airport combinations. Using Bureau of Transportation
Statistics on-time data from 2015--2024, NOAA GSOM monthly weather summaries,
DFW hub-weather features, and derived duty/cascade proxies, we reconstruct
398,098 tail-matched sequences and train an XGBoost classifier calibrated via
isotonic regression to estimate disruption risk at the corridor-month level.
This framing turns the challenge from a generic airport-ranking exercise into a
decision-support problem grounded in actual operations.

Our final model achieves a validation AUC of \textbf{0.825} and Average
Precision of \textbf{0.445} on 2024 holdout data (11.4\% positive rate,
$\mathrm{AP}_{\text{lift}} = 3.90\times$ above random), with calibrated scores
ranging from 1.2\% to 58.3\% and tracking observed disruption rates closely.
We then deploy the result in a Streamlit dashboard with pair-level
explanations, live schedule views, and a Hungarian-algorithm optimizer that
constructs lower-risk DFW arrival--departure matches for operational use.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Problem Context}
Dallas/Fort Worth International Airport (DFW) is the center of American
Airlines' domestic hub-and-spoke operation. On a normal day, this structure is
an advantage: aircraft, crews, and passengers can be routed efficiently through
a common hub, allowing a large national network to function as a connected
system. During bad weather, however, the same connectivity becomes a source of
fragility. A thunderstorm bank at one spoke airport, a line of convective
weather over North Texas, or a low-visibility event at a destination can ripple
through multiple flights that were originally scheduled to fit together tightly.

That ripple matters especially for pilot and crew sequencing. In practice,
crews do not operate isolated flights; they operate chains of flights whose
timing, legality, and reliability depend on earlier legs finishing close to
plan. When an inbound leg arrives late into DFW, the outbound leg may inherit
that delay directly through aircraft availability, gate pressure, or reduced
turn time. What begins as a local weather issue can therefore become an
operational sequence problem: a crew arrives late, departs late, risks missing
connections, and may face tighter duty-time constraints for the rest of the
day. For an airline, these disruptions are costly not only in minutes of delay,
but also in reassignment effort, downstream network instability, and degraded
schedule reliability.

This project is motivated by a simple operational question: before these
sequences are built, can we identify combinations of flights that are
structurally more vulnerable to weather-related disruption? More specifically,
if a crew is scheduled to operate an inbound flight from airport $A$ into DFW
and then an outbound flight from DFW to airport $B$, are there origin--
destination combinations that should be avoided because they repeatedly create
fragile pairings? Our goal is not to predict the weather itself. Rather, it is
to use historical flight and weather patterns to quantify which DFW-linked
sequences tend to become risky under seasonal and operational stress.

\subsection{Challenge Statement}
The EPPS--American Airlines Data Challenge asks teams to identify flights that
should not be assigned to the same pilot sequence when weather risk is high.
For the purposes of the competition, the full multi-day crew-routing problem is
simplified to a two-leg pattern centered on DFW: an inbound leg $A \to
\text{DFW}$ followed by an outbound leg $\text{DFW} \to B$, where $A$ and $B$
are domestic airports. The practical objective is to flag pairings that are
more likely to create cascading delays, missed turn opportunities, duty-time
pressure, and other operational disruptions.

At first glance, this sounds like a ranking problem over airport pairs. But the
challenge is harder than simply asking which airports have bad weather. Weather
is seasonal, disruption is sparse, and delay propagation depends on how flights
are actually connected in the operation. A pairing can look risky because both
airports are storm-prone, because DFW itself is vulnerable during certain
months, or because the timing of the rotation leaves little margin for recovery
once the first leg slips. The project therefore requires both domain judgment
and careful data construction: the central question is not only which features
matter, but also what the correct unit of analysis should be.

Our report treats a ``bad sequence'' as one that is operationally fragile rather
than merely inconvenient. The underlying idea is decision support: if American
can recognize these fragile pairings ahead of time, schedulers can avoid
placing them in the same crew sequence, add more buffer where needed, or at
least understand where the network is most exposed.

\subsection{Our Approach}
Our approach evolved in stages. We began with the intuitive idea of measuring
historical risk at the airport-pair level, reasoning that some combinations of
spoke airports would be more weather-sensitive than others. Very quickly,
however, we found that this view was too coarse. Airport pairs do not disrupt
operations on their own; real aircraft rotations do. That realization led to
the central methodological choice of the project: instead of fabricating
same-day pairings from all inbound and outbound flights, we reconstruct actual
DFW sequences by matching inbound and outbound legs on the same aircraft tail
number within a realistic turnaround window. This turns the problem from an
abstract corridor-ranking exercise into a model of real operational handoffs.

Once the sequence itself was defined correctly, the rest of the system followed
naturally. We combined historical BTS delay statistics, monthly weather
summaries, DFW hub conditions, and derived duty/cascade features into a
sequence-level feature set. We then trained a gradient-boosted classifier and
calibrated its outputs so that the resulting score can be interpreted as an
estimated disruption rate rather than an opaque ranking signal. Finally, we
translated the model into an operational tool through an interactive dashboard
and a sequence optimizer that selects lower-risk arrival--departure matches at
DFW.

The remainder of this report follows that journey. We first describe the data
sources and the sequence-construction problem, then explain the feature
engineering and modeling choices, present validation results, and conclude with
the decision-support tools that make the work actionable in practice.

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{We reframe the problem around real sequences.} Rather than scoring
        synthetic same-day airport combinations, we construct 398,098 tail-matched
        aircraft rotations from BTS data, giving the model a unit of analysis
        that reflects how disruption actually propagates through the DFW hub.
  \item \textbf{We build an interpretable disruption score.} Using XGBoost with
        isotonic calibration, we produce corridor-month risk estimates that are
        designed to be read as expected disruption rates, not just relative model
        scores.
  \item \textbf{We enrich weather risk with operational context.} In addition to
        airport-level weather history, the feature set incorporates DFW hub
        conditions, duty-proxy features, and cascade signals that capture how
        delay can compound across connected legs.
  \item \textbf{We turn prediction into decision support.} The final system is
        deployed as an interactive dashboard with pair-level explanations and a
        Hungarian-algorithm optimizer for constructing lower-risk DFW sequences.
  \item \textbf{We document the project's modeling lessons.} The report highlights
        why naive corridor-day construction overstates risk, how calibration
        improves operational usability, and where data limitations still matter.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Data Sources and Collection}
\label{sec:data}
% ============================================================

\subsection{BTS On-Time Performance Database}
The core of the project is the Bureau of Transportation Statistics (BTS)
On-Time Performance database, which provides flight-level records including
origin and destination airports, scheduled and actual departure/arrival times,
tail numbers, delay causes, cancellation codes, and late-aircraft propagation
fields. This source is what makes the project operationally meaningful: it lets
us observe not just where weather occurred, but how delays moved through actual
DFW-connected flights.

Rather than downloading the entire national network, we filtered BTS to flights
touching DFW, which keeps the dataset aligned with the challenge prompt while
preserving the local hub structure needed for sequence reconstruction. We use
all-carrier DFW traffic for modeling because airport-level weather fragility is
not unique to American Airlines; broader carrier coverage provides a richer
historical picture of how each airport behaves under stress. Within that
filtered scope, the most important columns are the flight date, tail number,
origin/destination, scheduled and actual times, weather delay, NAS delay,
arrival delay, and late-aircraft delay.

We use 2015--2019 and 2022--2024, excluding 2020--2021 because COVID-era
operations introduced structural distortions in schedules, route patterns, and
delay behavior. The year 2024 is reserved as the primary holdout period for
validation, while earlier years supply the historical signal used for training
and feature construction.

\subsection{NOAA Global Summary of the Month (GSOM)}
BTS tells us how disruption manifested, but it does not fully describe the
underlying weather environment at each airport. To add that broader seasonal
context, we augment the flight data with NOAA's Global Summary of the Month
(GSOM) dataset. GSOM is useful here because the challenge is not to forecast a
particular thunderstorm on a particular afternoon; it is to understand which
corridors are structurally vulnerable during certain months of the year.

For each covered airport, we collect monthly climate indicators such as average
wind speed, maximum wind gust, number of precipitation days, total
precipitation, and extreme precipitation. These features are averaged across
2015--2024 to form a climatological baseline by airport and month. In other
words, GSOM helps the model learn that Boston in January, Miami in September,
and Denver in March are exposed to different kinds of recurring weather stress
even before looking at any one day's operations.

Coverage is incomplete: only a subset of airports in the DFW network has a
mapped NOAA station with reliable monthly records, and roughly 55\% of sequence
rows are missing at least one GSOM-side input. Rather than treating that as a
reason to discard the features, we use GSOM as a secondary contextual layer and
later address the missing-data problem explicitly through median imputation and
calibration-aware interpretation.

\subsection{DFW Hub Weather}
Even when both spoke airports are relatively stable, the hub itself can become
the bottleneck. Because every modeled sequence passes through DFW, local hub
conditions deserve their own representation rather than being absorbed into the
origin and destination features. We therefore derive monthly DFW weather
statistics directly from BTS, including weather-delay rate, weather-cancellation
rate, average weather-delay minutes, and a high-percentile delay measure.

This distinction matters conceptually. Origin and destination features capture
how risky airport $A$ and airport $B$ tend to be; the DFW hub features capture
how much shared stress is present in the middle of every sequence. A corridor
can therefore be risky not only because its endpoints are volatile, but also
because the common transfer point is seasonally fragile.

\subsection{Data Gaps and Limitations}
The data used in this project are strong enough to support a meaningful
decision-support prototype, but they are not perfect. First, BTS is an
excellent source for realized delay and cancellation behavior, yet it is still
an administrative record of flights rather than a ground-truth record of crew
assignments. Tail matching provides a practical proxy for crew continuity, but
it does not capture every crew swap or recovery intervention.

Second, the weather data are intentionally asymmetric. BTS provides rich
evidence of disruption outcomes, while GSOM provides slower-moving monthly
climate context rather than flight-specific meteorology. This is a reasonable
tradeoff for the challenge, but it means the model should be interpreted as a
risk model for structurally fragile pairings, not as a deterministic flight-day
forecast. Third, GSOM coverage is incomplete, so missingness must be handled
carefully to avoid biasing the model toward better-stationed airports.

Finally, the interactive application includes optional live schedule and weather
components whose external APIs are subject to rate limits and availability
constraints. These do not affect the historical model results reported here,
but they do matter for deployment realism and are treated as implementation
limits rather than modeling limits.

\subsection{Tail-Matched Sequence Construction}
\label{sec:tailmatch}
% The core methodological contribution — explain why corridor-day is wrong,
% what tail-matching does, algorithm for linking legs

\begin{keybox}[title=Why Corridor-Day Cross-Join is Flawed]
The corridor-day approach cross-joins \emph{all} inbound flights $A \to \text{DFW}$
with \emph{all} outbound flights $\text{DFW} \to B$ on the same calendar date.
For a busy hub day with 8 inbound and 6 outbound flights, this fabricates
$8 \times 6 = 48$ ``sequences,'' none of which correspond to a real aircraft
rotation.  The label (``any flight disrupted'') then scales with hub volume,
teaching the model that hub size $\approx$ risk.
\end{keybox}

% Algorithm box for tail-matching
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Tail-Matched Sequence Construction}
\label{alg:tailmatch}
\KwIn{BTS flight table $F$ (DFW-filtered, 2015--2024)}
\KwOut{Sequence table $S$ with one row per real aircraft rotation}
$\text{IB} \gets \{f \in F : f.\texttt{Dest} = \text{DFW}\}$ \tcp*{inbound legs}
$\text{OB} \gets \{f \in F : f.\texttt{Origin} = \text{DFW}\}$ \tcp*{outbound legs}
Pre-rename flight-specific columns: \texttt{ArrDelay\_A}, \texttt{WeatherDelay\_A}, etc.\;
$S \gets \text{IB} \bowtie_{\texttt{Tail\_Number},\,\texttt{FlightDate}} \text{OB}$ \tcp*{inner join}
$\text{turnaround} \gets \texttt{CRSDepTime\_B} - \texttt{CRSArrTime\_A}$\;
$S \gets \{s \in S : 30 \le \text{turnaround} \le 240\}$\;
$S \gets \{s \in S : s.\texttt{Origin\_A} \ne s.\texttt{Dest\_B}\}$ \tcp*{no round-trips}
\Return $S$
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{GSOM NaN Imputation}
\label{sec:gsom_impute}
% 55% airports lack NOAA station
% Problem: XGBoost NaN routing learned "no station" ≈ lower risk
%   → ANC, ALB got artificially low scores
% Fix: month-level population medians from gsom_month_medians.json
%   filled at inference time in predictor.py
% SHAP UI: ★ marker + amber border on imputed bars

\begin{keybox}[title=GSOM Imputation Fairness Fix]
Without imputation, XGBoost's NaN routing learned a spurious pattern:
airports without NOAA stations (55\% of airports) were systematically
assigned lower risk scores because the NaN split coincidentally correlated
with smaller, calmer airports.  This was discovered when ANC (Anchorage) and
ALB (Albany) received near-zero calibrated scores despite operational evidence
of weather disruption risk.  We fix this by substituting month-level population
medians from a pre-computed lookup table at inference time.
\end{keybox}

\section{Sequence Optimizer}
\label{sec:optimizer}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Problem Statement}
% Given a day's schedule of inbound/outbound pairs at DFW,
% assign crew/aircraft to minimize total expected weather disruptions
% Integer assignment problem: each aircraft can only be in one sequence

\subsection{Hungarian Algorithm}
% Cost matrix: calibrated risk scores for all feasible (A→DFW→B) pairs
% scipy.optimize.linear_sum_assignment; O(n^3) complexity
% Feasibility constraints: turnaround window, aircraft availability

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{DFW Sequence Optimizer (Hungarian Algorithm)}
\label{alg:optimizer}
\KwIn{Inbound flights $I$, outbound flights $O$, risk model $f$}
\KwOut{Optimal assignment $A^* \subseteq I \times O$}
$C \gets \mathbf{0}^{|I| \times |O|}$ \tcp*{cost matrix}
\For{$(i, o) \in I \times O$}{
  \eIf{$30 \le \text{turnaround}(i,o) \le 240$}{
    $C[i, o] \gets f(i, o)$ \tcp*{calibrated risk score}
  }{
    $C[i, o] \gets \infty$ \tcp*{infeasible assignment}
  }
}
$(r^*, c^*) \gets \textsc{HungarianSolve}(C)$\;
\Return $A^* = \{(I[r^*_k], O[c^*_k])\}$
\end{algorithm}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Key Findings}
% Risk is driven by: seasonal timing (spring/summer), DFW hub congestion,
% origin airport weather profile, crew duty accumulation
% Tail-matched sequences: only 3.6 real rotations per pair-month on average
%   → confirms model was previously over-estimating frequency

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tail-number matching coverage.} Some rotations involve crew swaps
        or aircraft swaps that break the tail-chain; these are excluded.
        Estimated coverage: $\sim$65--70\% of actual crew sequences.
  \item \textbf{Calibration on full dataset.} Isotonic regression fitted on all
        398k rows (no holdout); calibration error on genuinely unseen
        route-months will be slightly higher.
  \item \textbf{GSOM 55\% NaN.} Month-level medians imputed for airports without
        NOAA stations; imputation noise is visible in SHAP output.
  \item \textbf{OOD validation pending.} Out-of-distribution tests were last run
        on v1 models; v7 OOD numbers are not yet available.
  \item \textbf{BTS reporting lag.} 2025 data not yet incorporated.
\end{itemize}
